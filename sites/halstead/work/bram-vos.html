Architecture practice, Rotterdam · 2023

Nine seconds to under one, at full resolution.

An architecture practice whose work is photographs, on a site that took nine seconds to show one. The people they most wanted to impress were opening it on a phone, on a building site, on bad signal.

01

The problem

Every image was a full-resolution JPEG, served at whatever size the browser asked for, through a page builder that loaded four carousel libraries to show one photograph. The first project image was 6.1MB.

Bram's objection was reasonable and had blocked every previous attempt: the images are the work, and he was not going to let anyone compress them into mush to win a score in a testing tool.

02

What we did

So we did not compress them into mush. We built a pipeline that generates seven widths per image in AVIF and WebP with a JPEG fallback, at quality settings chosen by eye — Bram sat with me and rejected settings until he could not tell the difference on his own monitor.

Then the page stopped fighting itself: no carousel library, no page builder, no fonts blocking the first paint. A photograph appears before anything else on the page, which for this practice is the entire point.

  • Seven responsive widths per image, AVIF/WebP/JPEG, generated on upload
  • Quality thresholds set by the architect, by eye, not by a score
  • Native lazy-loading below the fold; the hero image preloaded
  • A CMS the practice manager can use without a training session

03

How it was built

Static generation with an image pipeline that runs at build time, so an upload becomes twenty-one derivatives without anyone thinking about it. The CMS writes Markdown; the build does the rest.

No JavaScript is required to view a project. The gallery enhances with it and works completely without it, which is what makes it fast on the connection that actually matters.

04

What happened

Largest contentful paint went from 9.2 seconds to 0.9 on throttled 4G, with 87% fewer bytes and no change a working architect can see. Mobile pages per session more than doubled.

I said no to this three times because the last two people wanted to ruin the photographs. He proved he would not, then made it ten times faster anyway.Bram Vos, principal
